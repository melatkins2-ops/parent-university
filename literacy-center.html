        Understand your child's growth

        Questions parents often ask

        Reading develops over time. These starting points can help you understand what to notice and what to ask—not diagnose a problem or replace your child's teacher.

        What should my child be learning as a reader?

        Preschool–Grade 2: Listening and speaking, enjoying books, hearing sounds in words, connecting letters and sounds, and beginning to read and understand text.

        Grades 3–5: Reading with growing accuracy and understanding, learning new vocabulary, explaining main ideas, and using details from text.

        Grades 6–8: Understanding increasingly complex texts, comparing viewpoints, using evidence, and communicating ideas clearly.

        Grades 9–12: Evaluating sources and arguments, reading across subjects, and using evidence in writing and discussion.

        Children progress at different rates. Ask the teacher which specific skills your child is working on now.

        How can I help without making reading a struggle?

        Start with your child's interests. Read aloud, talk about what you notice, and let your child explain their thinking. If an activity becomes frustrating, shorten it or return to it another day.

        For children learning to read words, ask the teacher which letter-sound patterns have been taught so home practice reinforces classroom learning.

        My child can read the words but doesn't understand. What can I do?

        Talk before, during, and after reading. Explain unfamiliar words, connect the text to something your child knows, and ask them to show which part helped answer a question.

        If the difficulty continues, share specific examples with the teacher and ask what kind of support would help.

        What if my child doesn't enjoy reading?

        Offer choices: graphic novels, recipes, sports stories, biographies, instructions, magazines, and books about favorite topics. Read or listen together without turning every moment into a test.

        Ask your child what they would like to learn about, not just what book they want to read.

        What if our family speaks a language other than English?

        Keep telling stories, asking questions, reading, and having meaningful conversations in the language or languages your family knows best. Those experiences build important language and literacy foundations.

        Ask your school about translated materials, interpretation, and support for multilingual learners.

        Home and school together

        When you want more support

        You do not need to figure everything out on your own. Start with your child's teacher or school and describe what you notice at home.

        Three useful questions to ask

        • What is my child doing well in reading and writing right now?
        • What is one specific skill we should focus on next?
        • What is one realistic thing we can do at home, and how will we know it's helping?

        If you are concerned about ongoing difficulties, ask what school-based supports are available and how progress will be shared with you.
